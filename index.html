What is physically modelled, and what isn’t

  • Accurate: the geomagnetic field is an eccentric, tilted dipole. The pole is at 80.8°N, 72.6°W and the centre is offset about 570 km toward the western Pacific, which is what creates the South Atlantic Anomaly. Earth rotates at its sidereal rate, and the satellites fly circular Keplerian orbits.
  • Accurate: electrons come from the fission beta spectrum (Carter fit) and are emitted isotropically. Their L‑shells, equatorial pitch angles and mirror latitudes are computed from conservation of the first adiabatic invariant. Drift rates use the relativistic guiding‑centre formula with Hamlin’s pitch‑angle correction, so drift periods are real: about an hour for 1 MeV at L≈1.2.
  • Accurate: loss cones. Electrons that mirror below 100 km anywhere on their drift path are lost, either in their first bounce or when they drift over the SAA, where the field dips closest to Earth.
  • Approximate: slow decay from atmospheric scattering. The lifetime grows exponentially with the electrons’ lowest mirror altitude (about 1 day at 150 km, months at 500 km, capped at 5 years), roughly matching how the Starfish belt decayed.
  • Simplified: the debris cloud is a random spread of about 400 km horizontally, plus an upward rise of around 600 km (up to 4,000 km). That reproduces the L≈1.1–2 spread measured after Starfish, which a compact cloud at 400 km would not: its field lines dip into the atmosphere over the SAA. All electrons are injected at t = 0, although real debris keeps decaying for hours. Bounce motion is drawn slowed down, since real bounces take about 0.1 s. There are no solar storms or external field.
  • Real satellites: all 17,405 active, Earth‑orbiting satellites in CelesTrak’s catalogue of 20 Nov 2026, each with its true perigee, apogee and inclination, flown as a Kepler orbit with J2 precession of the node and perigee. The catalogue does not give positions along the orbit, so starting node and phase are random. Over the days to months that matter for dose this barely changes the result: the belt surrounds Earth within hours, and orbits precess through every longitude.
  • Electron inventory: 1.45×10²³ fissions per kiloton, assuming 50% of the yield comes from fission, times 0.40, 1.29 or 5.21 beta electrons per fission. Those are the low, central and high source cases of Petersen et al. (2026), with electrons released over the first 2 s, 20 s or six months. Late electrons are injected where the debris was at the burst; in reality the debris has spread out by then. Each simulated electron stands for about 10²² real ones.
  • Dose: each electron’s density along its field line, times its speed, gives the omnidirectional flux at each satellite. This is converted to dose in silicon behind 2 mm of aluminium. Electrons below about 0.9 MeV are stopped; above that, they deposit about 1.7 MeV·cm²/g. Bremsstrahlung, protons, the natural belts and solar cell damage are left out.
  • Validation: for Starfish, this field‑line model gives an average flux of about 4.5×10¹² electrons/cm² per day above 0.5 MeV along Telstar 1’s orbit over its first 200 days. Telstar measured about 6×10¹². The Princeton model spreads electrons over the whole magnetic shell and gives roughly 7× less dose in low orbit. Both are simplifications of the same physics, so the page offers both. The default scales dose by 0.15, which reproduces that study’s 1 Mt, 500 km South Atlantic benchmark: 2.7% killed in the flash (paper: 2.9%), 17% / 95% / 100% failed at 40 krad by days 1 / 3 / 7 (paper: about 25 / 78 / 98%), and 70% at 120 krad by day 7 (paper: about 72%).
  • Known gap at low yield: here the belt loses its low‑mirroring electrons, the ones that hit low orbits, faster than in the Princeton model, which includes radial diffusion and electron lifetimes of up to eight years. At 100 kt this page fails about 40% of low‑orbit satellites within a month at 40 krad, where the paper finds about 90%. At 10 kt it fails about 16% within a year, against the paper’s 92%. Read low‑yield results as a lower bound.
  • Thresholds: real tolerance varies enormously and isn’t published for most satellites. The presets are 10 krad (sensitive commercial parts), 40 and 120 krad (the civilian‑weighted and hardened blends used by Petersen et al.) and 300 krad (radiation‑hardened). The flash kills a satellite in line of sight if its X‑ray fluence exceeds 40 J/m², scaled up for thresholds above 40 krad, assuming 70% of the yield is emitted as X‑rays.
  • Density cloud: each simulated electron stands for about 10²² real ones with a spread of energies. It’s drawn as 24 soft blobs spread through its bounce phase, across about ±0.01 in L, and in drift longitude by ±50% of its drift rate times its age. That’s how far real electrons in such a bundle would have spread, so the cloud starts compact and wraps into a smooth shell over a few drift periods. Where blobs pile up and glow brighter, the electron density is higher.
  • The burst’s appearance follows what was seen and photographed in 1962. First there’s a white X‑ray and visible flash that lights up the view and the Earth below. Then a debris shell expands and cools from white to orange to red, and is drawn out along the magnetic field. X‑rays absorbed around 90 km heat a broad red patch of upper atmosphere beneath the burst. Debris and beta electrons stream down the field line through the burst and light up aurora at both of its ends, as at Samoa, about 3,000 km to the south, after Starfish. These effects play in wall‑clock time (about 90 s), whatever the speed setting. Sizes grow with the cube root of yield. For the first second a pale sphere shows the flash damage radius, then fades out, since the flash only does damage at the instant of the burst.
